# Information & Communication — Band B (Grades 1–2, ages 6–7)

**Core idea:** a message needs a sender and a receiver, and the same word can mean different things depending on the situation (context).

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Information & Communication - A PreK-K]] — turn-taking and signals are familiar; this band names the sender/receiver structure explicitly and introduces context through simple examples.

## Hands-on activities
- Play a simple secret-code game (e.g., tap codes, a basic substitution alphabet) — one child sends, another receives and decodes.
- Find a word that means two different things depending on the sentence (e.g., "bat" the animal vs. "bat" for baseball) and act out both.
- Telephone/whisper-down-the-lane game — notice how a message can change as it passes along.
- Identify who's the "sender" and who's the "receiver" in everyday situations (a text message, a wave hello).

## Draft

### Sender and Receiver

Before any game, just point at a few everyday moments and name the two roles out loud: when she waves hello, she's the sender and the other person is the receiver; when someone texts you, they're the sender and you're the receiver. Once that pair of words is familiar, it's ready to use as a tool in the games below rather than something you have to stop and explain mid-activity.

### Sending a Secret Code

Teach her one simple code — a tap code (one tap per letter's position, or a made-up tap-count for a short set of words), or a basic substitution ("A" means "go," two taps means "stop"). One of you is the sender, the other the receiver: the sender taps the message, the receiver has to decode it without seeing it written down. Swap roles. The lesson is implicit in the mechanics — a message only works if the receiver knows the same code the sender used, which is worth asking about directly: "What would happen if I used my code but you didn't know it?" This is the first hint at a much bigger idea — see [[Information & Communication - D Grades 6-8]] for how computers do the same thing with codes like binary.

### One Word, Two Meanings

Say the word "bat" and ask her to act out what it means — she'll probably pick one. Then ask if there's another "bat," and act out the other (the animal vs. the baseball bat). Try a few more ("bark," "trunk," "spring") and ask each time: "How do you know which one I mean?" The answer is context — the rest of the sentence, or what's happening around you, tells you which meaning is right even though the word itself didn't change. This is worth revisiting later as something that costs a small moment of mental work to sort out — see [[Information & Communication - C Grades 3-5]].

### Telephone

Line up three or more people (stuffed animals can fill in for a small family) and whisper a short sentence into the first ear. Pass it down the line, whisper to whisper, and compare what comes out the other end to what went in. It's almost always different — ask her to guess where in the chain it probably changed. Each sender/receiver pair along the line is doing the same job from the first activity above, just chained together, and each handoff is one more chance for the message to drift.
