# Life & Living Systems — Band B (Grades 1–2, ages 6–7)

**Core idea:** living things go through a life cycle (egg → caterpillar → butterfly, seed → sprout → plant), and we sense the world through five named senses.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Life & Living Systems - A PreK-K]] — the child has already watched a plant or pet grow; this band gives the growth stages names and orders them into a cycle.

## Hands-on activities
- Raise a butterfly from caterpillar (or watch a time-lapse) and draw each stage in order. Materials: backup butterfly time-lapse video queued offline; index cards + crayons.
- Chart the bean plant from Band A week by week: seed, sprout, leaves, flower. Materials: bean seed, clear cup, index cards + crayons.
- Blindfolded taste/smell/touch guessing game to name and isolate each of the five senses. Materials: blindfold, lemon slice, cracker, cotton ball, pinecone, bell, glass to tap.
- Compare a plant's "parts and jobs" (roots drink, leaves eat sunlight) to an animal's. Materials: a weed pulled up with roots intact (or a grocery-store bunch of radishes/green onions), a celery stalk with leaves, food coloring, a cup.

## Draft

### Watching Something Grow

This one isn't a single sit-down activity — it's a check-in you repeat over days or weeks, which is exactly the point: growth is the one phenomenon in this whole curriculum you can't demonstrate in a single sitting. Start a bean seed in a clear cup (so the roots show) or watch butterfly time-lapse footage together if a live caterpillar isn't on hand. Each time you check in, ask her to describe what changed since last time, before you tell her anything. Have her draw each stage on its own small card as it happens, rather than all at once from memory at the end — the real-time noticing matters more than a tidy finished chart.

Once there are several cards, lay them out in order and ask her to name each stage: seed, sprout, leaves, flower (or egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly). Then ask the question that turns a line into a cycle: "What happens after the flower? After the butterfly?" A flower makes seeds. A butterfly lays eggs. The line of cards loops back around to the first card — that's the actual shape of a life cycle, not a straight path from small to big.

While you're looking at the plant, it's a natural moment to compare it to an animal she knows well — a pet, or herself. Roots drink water the way a mouth does; leaves "eat" sunlight the way a mouth eats food. Different-looking parts, same job. Save the deeper version of "what are living things actually built from" for later — this band is just about noticing that living things have parts with jobs, and that those jobs repeat in a cycle (see [[Life & Living Systems - C Grades 3-5]] for where "parts" gets a much smaller, much more interesting answer).

### The Five Senses Game

Blindfold her (or just have her close her eyes) and hand her a sequence of safe household objects and foods to identify one sense at a time: smell a lemon slice, taste a cracker, touch a cotton ball vs. a pinecone, listen to a ringing bell vs. a tapped glass. After each guess, ask which sense she used to figure it out, and name it — don't pre-announce "now we're testing smell," let her notice which sense she's reaching for.

Close by asking her to name all five without you listing them first. If she gets stuck on one, that's useful information, not a problem to smooth over — it tells you which sense to lean on next time you're doing Band A-style noticing together.

### A Plant's Parts and Their Jobs

Unlike the other two lessons here, this one is a single sitting, start to finish — no days of waiting for anything to happen. Pull up a weed from the yard, roots and all, or use a grocery-store bunch of radishes or green onions (they keep their roots attached). A real plant with every part visible beats a diagram every time. Lay it on the table and ask her to point out what looks different before you name anything: let her notice root, stem, and leaf on her own first.

Then take each part in turn, question before answer. Roots: "The plant can't walk to the sink for a drink — how do you think it gets water?" (Roots pull water up out of the soil.) Leaves: "What does a leaf have that the other parts don't?" (It's flat and spread out, built to catch sunlight.) Stem: it's the hallway in between — water travels up through it to reach the leaves. If there's time, make that last part visible: stand a leafy celery stalk in a cup of water tinted with food coloring and check back in a few hours — the color climbing the stalk is the stem's job, caught in the act.

Now flip it onto her: "You don't have roots or leaves — so how do you get water, and how do you get your energy?" Her mouth drinks the way roots do; eating is how she gets energy the way a leaf gets its energy from sunlight. Different-looking parts, same two jobs. Close by asking her to put one shared job into her own words.

If "Watching Something Grow" already ran this visit, call back to it explicitly: point at the sprouted seed and ask "which part came up first?" (usually the root) and "why would the root need to grow before anything else?" — that ties this lesson to the growth check-in instead of leaving the two side by side as unrelated activities. This is also the direct ancestor of the much smaller "parts with jobs" answer waiting in [[Life & Living Systems - C Grades 3-5]] — cells — though that word has no business coming up yet.
