# Life & Living Systems — Band C (Grades 3–5, ages 8–10)

**Core idea:** living things are built from tiny building blocks called cells, and living things depend on each other in food chains and ecosystems.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Life & Living Systems - B Grades 1-2]] — life cycles and body parts are already familiar; this band adds the cell as the "tiny building block," explicitly paired with [[Matter & Energy - C Grades 3-5]]'s atoms/molecules as the same kind of idea one level up.

## Hands-on activities
- Look at onion skin or a cheek-swab cell under a cheap microscope or magnifier; sketch what's seen.
- Build a simple food chain from a local ecosystem (e.g., grass → rabbit → fox) with pictures or drawings.
- Introduce a basic human body system (digestive or circulatory) as a "machine made of parts with jobs," echoing the plant-parts-and-jobs idea from Band B.
- Explicitly name the parallel: "a cell is to your body what an atom is to water" — flag it, let the child sit with it.

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Cell** — one of the tiny building pieces a living thing is made of, invisible without magnification.
- **Food chain** — a sequence of living things where each one is food for the next.
- **Ecosystem** — a set of living things in one place, depending on each other to survive.
- **Body system** — a group of parts working together inside a body toward one job, like breaking down food.

Held back for later: *food web* (the fuller picture of dependencies a food chain is a simplified slice of) — arrives in [[Life & Living Systems - D Grades 6-8]]. This band draws a single line of who-eats-whom; the tangle of lines connecting to each other waits until she's seen enough single lines to want the bigger picture.

## Draft

### Looking at a Cell

Using a cheap magnifier or basic microscope, look at a thin slice of onion skin (easiest to prepare) or cells from a gentle cheek swab, and have the child sketch what they see. Most expect either nothing visible or something much bigger — the surprise that there's structure at all, invisible until magnified, is the point. Ask: "if your whole body is made of millions of these, what do you think each one is doing?" No complete answer needed yet — the question is doing the work.

### Grass, Rabbit, Fox

Build a simple food chain with drawings or pictures from a real local ecosystem — grass, a rabbit that eats the grass, a fox that eats the rabbit. Ask at each link: "what happens to this one if the one before it disappears?" (No grass, no rabbits; no rabbits, hungry foxes.) This sets up — without naming it yet — the idea that a chain is really a web of dependencies, which [[Life & Living Systems - D Grades 6-8]] widens into something closer to the real picture.

### A Machine Made of Parts

Pick one body system — digestive or circulatory, since both have an obvious job — and walk through its main parts as "a machine made of parts with jobs," the same framing as the plant-parts lesson from Band B. For digestion: mouth breaks food up, stomach and intestines break it down further, useful parts get absorbed. Ask the child to guess each part's job before you tell them, based only on where it sits in the sequence.

### The Same Idea, One Size Down

Say directly: "a cell is to your body what an atom is to a cup of water." Let that sit without over-explaining — a cup of water is built from countless identical tiny pieces too small to see that combine to make the whole; a body is built from countless specialized tiny pieces that combine to make the whole. Ask what's the same and what's different — the "different" answer (atoms in water are all identical; cells in a body come in many specialized types, skin and muscle and nerve cells all doing different jobs) is actually the more interesting finding.
