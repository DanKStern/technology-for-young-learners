# Matter & Energy — Band A (Pre-K–K, ages 3–5)

**Core idea:** stuff can look and feel very different (ice, water, steam) and still be "the same stuff" underneath — and some things can be mixed together.

**Spiral note:** first pass. Pure sensory noticing; no vocabulary for "states of matter" yet.

## Hands-on activities
- Watch an ice cube melt in a warm hand, then watch water boil (adult-led) and see steam.
- Mix two colors of paint or food coloring in water — notice a new color appears.
- Sort household objects by touch: squishy, hard, wet, dry, bumpy, smooth.
- Freeze a cup of water overnight, melt it back — "the same water went away and came back."
- Talk, sing, whisper, and shout into [[Voice Scope]] and watch a simple light bar climb and fall (green→yellow→red) with how loud she is; hum with fingers on her throat to feel the buzz the lights are showing.
- Listen to a handful of very different sounds (a water drip, a single flute note, a buzzy triangle wave, a buzzier square wave) and guess what each one's picture will look like before seeing it — and the other way around, guess what a picture will sound like before hearing it.

## Draft

### Ice, Water, Steam

Hand Mila an ice cube (on a plate or in a cloth, if she doesn't want wet fingers) and ask her to describe what she feels before you explain anything. Watch it shrink into a puddle over the next few minutes, asking "where did the ice go?" as it disappears, and really wait for an answer. Later — adult-led, her hands well back — let her watch a pot of water come to a boil and the steam rise off it: "is that the same stuff as the puddle on your hand?"

The payoff doesn't land in one sitting. A day or two before or after, freeze a cup of water overnight together, then let it melt back on the counter the next day, asking each time, pointing at it: "is this still water?" What she should walk away noticing, in her own words if possible, is that it's "the same stuff" whether it's hard, wet, or disappeared into the air — one thing changing clothes, not three different things. The actual names for those changes of clothes — solid, liquid, gas — are waiting for her at [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2]].

### Mixing Colors

Put out two cups of water, each with a different food coloring (or two blobs of paint on a plate), and let her do the mixing herself. Ask "what color do you think this will turn?" before each mix, not after — a wrong guess is often more interesting than a right one. Point out, gently, that once mixed, the two original colors can't easily be pulled back apart — worth noticing now, even without a name for it, since "can I undo this?" becomes the actual tool she's handed at [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2]].

A digital, gamified version of the same idea — [Mix-It Lab](https://dankstern.github.io/becca-arcade/mix-it-lab.html) — is good for extra practice between sessions, not a replacement for the real paint.

### Sorting by Touch

Gather objects with very different textures — a cotton ball, a rock, a wet sponge, a dry cracker, a pinecone, a smooth spoon — and let her sort them into her own piles with her own labels (squishy, hard, wet, dry, bumpy, smooth) rather than yours. Ask her to explain a grouping that surprises you rather than correcting it — her reasoning matters more here than whether it matches an adult's sort.

### Seeing Your Voice

*Revised 2026-11-01 after Becca's first real session: a scrolling voice-shaped line graph read as too abstract a starting point. A plain light bar that climbs with loudness is a more direct, concrete display for this age — closer to "the lines show something about your voice" being a question of effort rather than a question of reading a time-series graph.*

*(Needs a light-bar/VU-meter display mode on [[Voice Scope]] in place of the scrolling line — not built yet.)*

Open Voice Scope and press Start yourself — at this age the grown-up runs the buttons, and the animal buttons can wait for a later band. Don't say what the screen is. Just invite her to talk, sing, or make any noise she likes, and let her discover that the lights climb when she does. Ask "what do you think makes the lights go up?" and wait for her answer, whatever it is.

Then make a game of it: "can you make it reach all the way up to red? Now can you make it so quiet it barely tips the first green light?" Quiet is often harder and more fun than loud. Finally, have her hum while resting her fingers lightly on the front of her throat — she'll feel a buzz. "Your throat is wiggling, and the lights are showing us the wiggles." No word for vibration yet; that's waiting at [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2]]. Stop while she's still asking to do it again.

### Guess the Sound

*(Needs a small library of canned sounds on [[Voice Scope]] beyond the current single test tone — not built yet.)*

Play a handful of very different sounds one at a time — a water drip, a single flute note, a buzzy triangle wave, a buzzier square wave — and before each one shows its picture, ask "how do you think this will look?" Let her guess freely; there's no wrong answer. Then play it and watch the screen together.

Reverse it for a second round: show a picture first with the sound hidden, and ask "what do you think this will sound like?" This direction is harder and depends on what sounds she already has words and memories for — a drip or a flute note she can guess at, a wiggle she's never heard matched to a sound before she may not yet. Follow her lead on which sounds she has vocabulary for rather than working through a fixed list.
