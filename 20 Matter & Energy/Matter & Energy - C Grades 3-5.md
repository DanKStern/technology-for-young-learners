# Matter & Energy — Band C (Grades 3–5, ages 8–10)

**Core idea:** everything is made of tiny building blocks (atoms/molecules) too small to see, and there's a real difference between a physical change (ice to water) and a chemical change (baking soda + vinegar).

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2]] — states of matter and reversibility are already familiar; this band explains *why* some changes are reversible (same molecules, rearranged) and some aren't (new molecules made). Sets up the explicit cross-strand parallel to [[Life & Living Systems - C Grades 3-5]], where cells play the same "tiny building block" role for living things.

## Hands-on activities
- Mix baking soda and vinegar; watch the fizzing and feel the temperature change — evidence a chemical change happened.
- Build a simple ball-and-stick model of water (2 small balls + 1 bigger ball) to make "molecule" concrete.
- Melt and refreeze ice again, but this time frame it as "the same molecules, just rearranged" — contrast with the baking-soda reaction, where new molecules formed.
- Introduce hot/cold and simple insulators (why a mitten keeps a hand warm) as a first touch of energy transfer.

## Draft

### Fizzing Proof

Mix baking soda and vinegar in a cup and have the child feel the outside of the container as it fizzes — it usually cools slightly. Ask: "did we just make something new, or is this still baking soda and vinegar, just mixed?" The fizzing gas and the temperature change are both evidence that something genuinely new formed — carbon dioxide that wasn't in either ingredient before. Contrast this directly with melting and refreezing a cup of ice: run that demo again and ask the same question. This time the answer is the same water, just rearranged — which is the actual distinction this band exists to draw. Physical changes rearrange what's already there; chemical changes make something new.

### Building a Water Molecule

Using two small balls and one larger ball, build a simple model of a water molecule — two smaller atoms attached to one bigger one. Explain that everything, including the water and vinegar from the last activity, is built from atoms like these, just too small to ever see directly. Ask the child to guess: in the baking-soda reaction, did the atoms disappear, or just get rearranged into new combinations? (Rearranged — atoms are never created or destroyed in a chemical change; what's new is the combination, not the raw material.)

### Why a Mitten Keeps You Warm

Hand the child a mitten and ask: "does the mitten make heat, or does it do something else?" Most will say it makes you warm — push back gently by asking what happens if an already-cold hand goes into a mitten in the freezer. A mitten doesn't create heat; it slows heat from leaving your hand, by trapping air that doesn't conduct heat well. This is a first, concrete touch of energy transfer — heat moves from warm things to cold things unless something slows it down — that gets its full treatment once [[Matter & Energy - D Grades 6-8]] traces an entire energy story end to end.
