# Computing & Logic — Band A (Pre-K–K, ages 3–5)

**Core idea:** some things need to happen in a specific order (a step-by-step recipe, a dance), and it's hard to do two things perfectly at the same time.

**Spiral note:** first pass. Pure physical experience of sequencing and the limits of doing two things at once; no "algorithm" vocabulary yet.

## Hands-on activities
- Follow a simple picture recipe (make a sandwich, build a block tower) one step at a time, out of order first to see what breaks, then in order.
- Try patting your head and rubbing your belly at the same time — notice how tricky it is.
- A simple call-and-response dance with ordered steps.
- Unplugged "robot" game — give a grown-up step-by-step spoken directions to walk across the room.

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Step** — one single action in a sequence.
- **Order** — the sequence steps happen in, one after another.
- **At the same time** — two things happening together, neither one before the other.
- **Directions** — spoken words that describe exactly what to do.

Held back for later: *if/then rule* and *sharing by taking turns* — both arrive by name in [[Computing & Logic - B Grades 1-2]]. This band names only the raw experience of order and simultaneity; the formal rules for handling them come once she's felt enough of each to want a name for it.

## Draft

### Out of Order

Pick something Mila already knows how to do in steps — making a sandwich, building a block tower, getting dressed — and act it out with her completely out of order first (shoes on before socks, roof block stacked before the walls exist). Let the mess be the point; ask "did that work?" and let her tell you what went wrong before you say anything. Then do it again in the right order and ask her to compare.

### Two Things at Once

Try patting your head and rubbing your belly at the same time together, and let her feel how hard it is even though each one alone is easy. This is purely physical noticing at this age — no explanation needed, just enough repetition that "doing two things at once is hard" lands as a felt fact rather than something you told her.

### Robot Game

Be the "robot": tell her you'll do exactly what she says, no more and no less, and have her give you spoken directions to walk across the room or reach something. Follow her instructions totally literally, including any gaps she leaves ("walk forward" straight into a wall if she doesn't say to stop or turn). The resulting silliness is the lesson, and it's the direct ancestor of the same exercise waiting in [[Computing & Logic - B Grades 1-2]], where the gaps in a set of written instructions get taken seriously as a real problem to solve.
