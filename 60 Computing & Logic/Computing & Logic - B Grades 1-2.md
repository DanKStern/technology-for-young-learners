# Computing & Logic — Band B (Grades 1–2, ages 6–7)

**Core idea:** order matters in instructions, simple rules can be "if this, then that," and taking turns is a simple way to share something between two people.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Computing & Logic - A PreK-K]] — step-by-step sequencing is already familiar; this band names "if/then" rules explicitly and introduces turn-taking as a first, very simple answer to "how do two things share one resource?"

## Hands-on activities
- Simple unplugged sequencing game: put scrambled instruction cards back in the right order.
- Play "if/then" rule games ("if it's raining, bring an umbrella"; "if the light is red, stop") and invent a few together.
- Two children share one toy by taking timed turns — name this as one way to "share" something between two users.
- Build a simple instruction set for a sibling or stuffed animal to follow exactly as written.

## Draft

### Scrambled Instructions

Write out 4–5 steps of something she already knows how to do — making a sandwich, brushing teeth, getting dressed — one step per card. Shuffle the cards and hand them to her out of order. Ask her to follow them exactly as dealt, not in the order she knows is right, and watch what goes wrong (butter on bread before the bread exists, shoes before socks). Then let her physically re-sort the cards into working order and try again.

Once that lands, flip the exercise around: have her write (or dictate to you) a set of steps for you or a stuffed animal to follow — exactly as written, nothing assumed. Follow her instructions literally, including the gaps she didn't think to fill in ("put on the shirt" with no mention of which way is front — put it on backwards). The resulting mess is the lesson: a set of instructions only works if every step that matters is actually written down, in the right order.

### If This, Then That

Invent a few rules together in the form "if this, then that" — "if it's raining, bring an umbrella," "if the light is red, stop," "if you're thirsty, get water." Say the "if" half and have her supply the "then." Then reverse it: give her the "then" and have her invent a matching "if." Point out that these rules only fire when their "if" actually happens — nothing happens from a rule whose condition never comes true, which is worth letting her notice on her own with an invented silly one ("if a dinosaur knocks on the door...").

### Taking Turns With One Toy

Two kids (or you and her), one toy, and a visible timer — ten seconds each, strictly enforced, back and forth. Ask her afterward: "Did both of you get to use it, even though there's only one?" Name this directly as *sharing by taking turns* — one resource, two users, solved by trading off instead of splitting the toy in half. This is the very first, simplest version of an idea that gets a real engineering answer much later — see [[Computing & Logic - C Grades 3-5]] for the same idea applied to a busy parent (or a computer) doing several things "at once," and [[Computing & Logic - D Grades 6-8]] for how a single wire actually shares itself between many phone calls.
