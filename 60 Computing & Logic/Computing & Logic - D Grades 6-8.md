# Computing & Logic — Band D (Grades 6–8, ages 11–13)

**Core idea:** a single wire can carry many separate conversations at once by splitting either by time (time-division multiplexing) or by frequency (frequency-division multiplexing) — the formal engineering version of "taking turns" from Band B and "doing many things at once" from Band C.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Computing & Logic - C Grades 3-5]] — algorithms, loops, and multitasking-as-fast-switching are already familiar; this band names the two concrete multiplexing strategies and connects explicitly back to [[Information & Communication - D Grades 6-8]]'s bandwidth/sharing-a-channel lesson — this is the original seed concept this whole curriculum grew from.

## Hands-on activities
- Time-division demo: several children "share" one marker, each getting a fixed number of seconds to draw before passing it on — that's time-division multiplexing.
- Frequency-division demo: play two different-pitched tones (or walkie-talkie channels) at once and show both can be received separately by filtering for pitch — that's frequency-division multiplexing. [[Voice Scope]] can do the filtering.
- Revisit Band B's "two kids share one toy by taking turns" and Band C's multitasking parent, and explicitly name both as early, informal versions of this band's two formal strategies.
- Research (or watch a short video on) how old telephone systems carried many calls on one wire — the real-world case this whole strand traces back to.

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Time-division multiplexing** — sharing one resource among many users by giving each a fixed time slot, in turn.
- **Frequency-division multiplexing** — sharing one resource among many users at the same time, separated by frequency instead of by turn.

## Draft

### Sharing a Marker by the Clock

Give several kids one marker and a strict timer — five seconds each, in a fixed rotation, no exceptions — and have them pass it around drawing on a shared piece of paper. Ask afterward: "did everyone get a fair turn, even though only one person could draw at any instant?" Name this precisely as time-division multiplexing — one resource, divided into time slots, each user getting their slot in turn. This is the exact same shape as the Band B toy-sharing lesson and the Band C multitasking parent, just with a formal name and a strict clock attached.

### Two Tones, One Air

Play two different-pitched tones at the same time (two phones, two instruments, or two walkie-talkies on different channels) and ask whether the child can tell both are happening, and whether they can pick out just one if they try. Explain that a radio or a filter can do exactly that on purpose — separate signals by pitch (frequency) rather than by turn — and name this frequency-division multiplexing: many signals sharing the same space at the same time, separated by frequency instead of by turns.

To actually do the separating, use [[Voice Scope]] as the filter. Play a low tone (around 200 Hz) from one phone and a high tone (around 2000 Hz) from another — any free tone-generator app or web page works — and record both at once. On playback, switch on only Bear: just the low tone comes through. Switch on only Bird: just the high one. Two "calls" in the same air, recorded together, pulled apart cleanly by frequency — the same trick the phone company used on a wire, and the same animal buttons from [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2#Which Animal Lives in Your Voice?|Band B]], now doing an engineering job.

### The Original Problem

Go back to where this whole strand started: old telephone systems needed to carry hundreds of people's phone calls over the same physical wire, and neither "wait your turn" nor "everyone talks over everyone else" was good enough. Ask the child, now that they have both tools: "which of our two tricks would you use, or would you use both?" (Real systems have used both, at different points in telephone history.) Close by connecting this all the way back: time-division here is the formal version of Band B's toy-sharing timer; frequency-division is a genuinely new trick, closer to the color-filter radiometer lesson back in Forces & Motion Band B, where different colors (frequencies) of light behaved differently through the same cellophane filter.
