# Systems, Hierarchy & Nesting — Band B (Grades 1–2, ages 6–7)

**Core idea:** groups can have groups inside them (a folder inside a folder), and a family has a shape too (parents, grandparents, siblings).

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Systems, Hierarchy & Nesting - A PreK-K]] — nesting toys become nested real-world categories; this band adds the idea that a category can itself contain smaller categories, not just objects.

## Hands-on activities
- Organize a real toy bin into labeled sub-bins (animals → dogs, cats; vehicles → cars, trucks).
- Draw a simple family tree with the child at the bottom and parents/grandparents above.
- Find a physical folder-inside-a-folder-inside-a-drawer at home and trace the path out loud.
- Play "20 questions" style narrowing games (animal → has fur → is it a dog?).

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Nesting** — a group that contains smaller groups, which can themselves contain smaller groups still.
- **Category** — a named kind of group that things get sorted into.
- **Generation** — one layer of a family, such as parents or grandparents, all at the same distance from the child.
- **Narrow down** — removing some of the remaining possibilities with each new piece of information.

Held back for later: *path* (the written list of nested steps needed to reach one specific thing) and *classification* (a formal, agreed-on nesting system) — both arrive by name in [[Systems, Hierarchy & Nesting - C Grades 3-5]].

## Draft

### Folders in Folders

Find a real folder-inside-a-folder situation at home — a filing drawer with folders inside it, or a bigger box holding labeled smaller boxes — and open it together layer by layer, naming each one out loud: "this is inside this, which is inside this." Then hand Becca a messy toy bin and ask her to invent her own sub-bins inside it — an animals bin containing a dogs pile and a cats pile, a vehicles bin containing cars and trucks. Then ask directly: "Can a group have groups inside it, the same way a box can have boxes inside it?" Let her answer from what she just built, not from being told.

### Her Family Tree

Draw a simple family tree together, Becca at the bottom, her parents above her, her grandparents above them. Before filling in each new layer, ask: "Whose mom and dad do you think goes up here?" Point out that this is the same "inside of inside" shape from the toy bins, just applied to people — the tree only makes sense read one layer at a time, bottom to top. If she's ready for a bit more, mention that she's also *inside more than one group at once* — her family, her class, her street — the same way a toy can be both "red" and "a truck." Better to let her discover that on her own than to assert it outright.

### Twenty Questions

Pick a secret animal (or object) and let her ask yes/no questions that narrow it down step by step — "does it have fur?" → "is it a pet?" → "is it a dog?" Each question should split the remaining possibilities into a smaller group than the question before it. Afterward, ask her to describe what she was doing in her own words: "How did asking about fur help you guess faster than just naming animals?" This same shape — a big category splitting into smaller ones until you land on one thing — becomes real classification (file paths, biological categories) at [[Systems, Hierarchy & Nesting - C Grades 3-5]], and class hierarchies in code at [[Systems, Hierarchy & Nesting - D Grades 6-8]].
