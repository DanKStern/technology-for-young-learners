# Forces & Motion — Band D (Grades 6–8, ages 11–13)

**Core idea:** Newton's laws explain motion qualitatively, and friction is really energy being converted to heat — energy doesn't disappear, it changes form.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Forces & Motion - C Grades 3-5]] — now the measured, named effects get a mechanism: force causes acceleration, and "lost" energy to friction is conserved as heat, setting up the energy-conservation idea that [[Matter & Energy - D Grades 6-8]] picks up from the chemistry side.

## Hands-on activities
- Feel a surface get warm after rubbing two objects together fast — that warmth *is* the "lost" kinetic energy.
- Qualitative Newton's-laws demo: push a cart on a low-friction track vs. a high-friction one, same push — compare how far each travels.
- Revisit the Band C friction/electrical-resistance analogy explicitly and push it further: both are a force resisting a flow (motion vs. current), both turn some of that flow into heat.
- Introduce momentum conceptually with a collision demo (two carts, one at rest).
- Revisit the Band B radiometer demo and go through the real debate: Crookes' original "light pressure" theory (wrong — disproven by Schuster and Lebedev), the currently-accepted thermal transpiration theory (Reynolds, 1879), and a newer competing "transimpact"/electrostatic theory (Liu, Stanford) that explains some anomalies thermal transpiration can't. Good concrete lesson that even settled-seeming science can still be an open question 150 years later.

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Energy conservation** — the fact that energy changes form rather than disappearing when motion stops.
- **Momentum** — motion that transfers between objects in a collision rather than vanishing.
- **Thermal transpiration** — the currently accepted explanation for the radiometer's spin: gas molecules moving differently off a warm surface than a cool one.
- **Radiation pressure** — the original explanation for the radiometer's spin, later disproven: light itself pushing directly on the vanes.

## Draft

### Where Did the Energy Go?

Have the child rub their hands together fast for ten seconds and notice the warmth, then ask directly: "That ball you dropped back in Band C eventually stopped bouncing. Where did its energy go?" Most kids will shrug or guess it disappeared. Point back at their own warm hands: the energy didn't disappear, it turned into heat — the same way the sliding, rubbing surfaces in the friction-ranking activity were quietly heating up the whole time, just too little to feel by hand. This is energy conservation stated the way a middle-schooler can actually use it: energy changes form, it doesn't vanish — a principle [[Matter & Energy - D Grades 6-8]] picks up again from the chemistry side.

### Same Push, Different Track

Set up two tracks for a toy cart or ball — one smooth, one rough or carpeted — and give the cart the same push on each, released from the same point. Ask before each run: "same push both times — will it go the same distance?" It won't. Tie this back to the electrical-resistance echo raised in Band C: both friction and electrical resistance are a force resisting a flow (motion, or electric current), and in both cases some of that flow's energy turns into heat along the way — a wire that resists current gets warm for the same underlying reason a dragged cart does.

### A Collision of Carts

Roll one cart into a second, identical cart sitting still, ideally with carts that can link or bounce apart cleanly. Ask the child to predict the outcome before each run: does the first cart stop completely? Does the second take off at the same speed? This is a concrete, ungraded introduction to momentum — motion that has to go somewhere when things collide — without needing the formula yet.

### The Radiometer, Revisited for Real

Pull the Band B radiometer back out and run it again — light, color filters, cupped hands, the freezer — but this time go through the actual history of the explanation. Crookes himself, in 1873, thought light was literally pushing the vanes (radiation pressure); tell the child this was a real scientist's real published theory, and that it was wrong, disproven a few years later by Schuster and Lebedev. The explanation accepted since 1879 (Reynolds' thermal transpiration) is about gas molecules behaving differently at the warm black edge than the cool white edge — the black side heating up faster, as [[Matter & Energy - D Grades 6-8]] traces, is what starts the whole chain. Then tell them a newer theory (Liu, at Stanford) argues thermal transpiration alone doesn't explain everything observed, and proposes an electrostatic effect instead — meaning this "settled" 1870s toy might still not be fully explained, a century and a half later. Close by asking: "Does it bother you that scientists still argue about something this simple-looking, or is that kind of exciting?" Either answer is the right one.
