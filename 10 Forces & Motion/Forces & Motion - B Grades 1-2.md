# Forces & Motion — Band B (Grades 1–2, ages 6–7)

**Core idea:** the falling thing has a name — gravity — and the slowing-down thing has a name — friction.

**Spiral note:** builds on [[Forces & Motion - A PreK-K]] — the child has already seen both effects; this band gives them words and lets them predict.

## Hands-on activities
- Drop the same ball from a low height and a high height — ask "what do you think will happen?" before each drop.
- Compare the same toy car on sandpaper vs. a smooth board; name the difference "friction."
- Try to slide a book across the table, then try again with the table wet — notice friction changes.
- Shine a flashlight on a Crookes radiometer (the glass bulb with the spinning black-and-white vanes) and watch it spin — ask "what do you think is pushing it?" Move the light closer/farther and count spins in 10 seconds to connect brightness to speed. Try holding colored cellophane between the flashlight and the bulb and see which colors speed it up more. Cup your hands around the glass (no touching) to show it can even spin from body heat alone, no light needed. Hold it near a cold pack or briefly in the freezer and watch it reverse — a fun surprise. This is really the same "invisible force" idea as gravity and friction, just a new one: light and heat can push things too. The real explanation is still debated by physicists and gets revisited in [[Forces & Motion - D Grades 6-8]].

## Vocabulary

Words this band uses, defined the way we'd describe them to someone who'd never seen them — what happens, not how it feels:

- **Gravity** — the pull that brings an object toward the ground.
- **Friction** — the resistance between two touching surfaces that slows their movement against each other.

Held back for later: the radiometer's spin stays unnamed in this band — no word is given for what pushes it, since the mechanism is still debated among physicists and gets its real names (*thermal transpiration*, *radiation pressure*) in [[Forces & Motion - D Grades 6-8]]. The next words this strand formally names, *inclined plane* and *lever*, arrive in [[Forces & Motion - C Grades 3-5]].

## Draft

### The Radiometer: An Invisible Push

Set the radiometer on the table between you, out of direct light, and let it sit still for a moment. Let Becca look at it first without any explanation — four little paddles inside a glass bulb, black on one side, silvery-white on the other, just hanging there. Ask her what she thinks it is. Toy? Ornament? Let her guess before you do anything.

Now turn on the flashlight and shine it on the bulb. The paddles start to spin. Give her a second to react before you say anything — the surprise is the point. Then ask the question that drives the whole lesson: "What do you think is pushing it? I'm not touching it, and neither are you." She already knows two things can push without touching — she's felt gravity pull a dropped ball down, and friction slow a sliding toy (both from earlier in this strand). This is a third one, and it's her job to notice that before you name it.

Move the flashlight closer, then farther away, and count how many spins happen in ten seconds each time. Closer and brighter makes it spin faster — that's the first real clue: whatever is pushing it, there's more of it when there's more light.

Now hand her a few pieces of colored cellophane and let her hold each one between the flashlight and the bulb in turn. Ask her to guess which color will make it spin fastest before she tries it, then compare. Darker colors (red, blue) tend to block more and slow it down more than a lighter one — a first hint that color changes how much gets through, which connects straight back to "black heats up faster than white" waiting in [[Matter & Energy - B Grades 1-2]].

Then take the flashlight away entirely and just cup your hands around the glass bulb, not touching it, for ten or fifteen seconds. It starts spinning again, slower, with no light at all. This is the twist: it's not really about light. It's about something the light was doing that your warm hands can also do.

Let her sit with that for a second, then ask: "If it's not the light itself, what do you think the light was actually doing?" You're not looking for the right answer — you're planting the idea that light can turn into something else before it does anything.

Finally, hold the bulb near a cold pack, or set it in the freezer for a minute with the door cracked open. When you take it out, it spins the other way for a little while before stopping. This is the best surprise of the three, and worth lingering on: cold makes it spin backwards.

Close the lesson by naming what happened, but only loosely — this band doesn't need the real mechanism (that's a Band D conversation, not a six-year-old's): "Gravity pulls things down. Friction slows things down when they rub. And now we've found a third one — light and heat can push something, even from across the room, even without touching it at all." Leave the "why" open. If she asks why it spins backwards when it's cold, =="that's one of the best questions in all of science — grown-up scientists still argue about the exact answer" is a true and satisfying thing to tell a six-year-old, and sets up revisiting it for real once she's older (see [[Forces & Motion - D Grades 6-8]]).==
